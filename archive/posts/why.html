Why

15/06/2026

There's a question I keep coming back to, particularly recently but aslo as I get older I seem to think about things in more depth than I did before.

Why?

Not "why technology" or "why engineering" — those feel like the wrong level of abstraction. More like: why do this at all? Why keep choosing the hard version of things when an easier version exists? Why does the bit that makes most people anxious — the blank page, the new team, the problem nobody's solved yet — feel like exactly the right place to be?

I don't have a complete answer. But I have a working theory.

The reading prize

I won a reading prize at school when I was around 12. I've forgotten the name of it. What I remember is that I'd already read everything I could find — not because I was told to, but because books made the world larger. I wanted to understand things. All of it. How things worked, why they broke, what happened if you changed one thing, why does THAT work like THAAAAAT.

That sort of curiosity never went away. I just found different things to attach it to, often things that were on the surface quite unproductive.

Most of it doesn't work

When I started getting deeper into technology, particularly software, I noticed most of it doesn't work properly. Not in a catastrophic the world will end kind of way way. In the way where you can see, clearly, that nobody really thought it through. That someone built the thing but didn't ask who was going to use it, or whether it was solving the right problem in the first place.

That was interesting to me. Not frustrating — interesting. Because interesting means there's room. There's something to fix, something to figure out, something to build properly.

I've spent the last decade or so trying to do that. Building products people actually use. Hiring people smarter than me in the areas they're smarter in. Getting things wrong and learning what not to do next time.

30 is the new 20 (apparently)

I'm 30-something now. I'm choosing to believe the above.

From where I'm standing, looking back at 22 or 25-year-old me, the thing I most want to be able to say is: I kept going. I didn't find a comfortable enough spot to stop. I stayed curious when I could have stopped being curious. I kept choosing the difficult version.

Paul Graham talks about doing what feels like play to you but looks like work to everyone else. Naval says something similar — that the thing that makes you irreplaceable is at the intersection of what you know, how you think, and who you are. No one wins a head-to-head against you being you.

I think about that a lot. Not as a strategy. As a compass.

What feels like play

The moment before the product exists. The team that isn't quite a team yet. The problem that doesn't have an obvious answer. The company that needs someone to make something from nothing.

What looks like work to everyone else: everything that comes with that. The ambiguity. The pressure. The days where nothing is clear and you have to make a call anyway.

The hard part is the point. Not because difficulty is noble. Because the hard things are where the growth is, and growth is the whole reason to do any of it. Every time is an education in what I don't know. Learning happens best under pressure.

What I'm building toward

I don't know exactly. I know it involves technology, and teams, and problems genuinely worth solving. I know I want to look back in ten years at something that didn't exist before I got there — and feel the distance between where something was when I arrived and where it ended up.

That's enough. More than enough.

On we go.
